The marsh had a memory, Mireth knew, longer than any living thing in Quill's Hollow cared
to admit. She had grown up on its edge — close enough that the smell of peat and black
water lived in the walls of her mother's house, close enough that the lantern in the window
each night was not decoration but defense. The older women said the marsh forgot nothing,
and the younger women laughed at them, and the older women did not laugh back.

She had been one of the younger women, once. She was not anymore.

The night the stranger came through, the reeds had been still for a full minute before he
appeared — and that was the first wrong thing. Reeds on the marsh edge never went still on
their own. Something had passed through them, or something was holding them. She had been
mending a net by the lamp, the door half-latched, when the silence arrived, and she had
known — not guessed, known — that she should not look up. So she looked up.

He was not tall. He was not armed in any way she could see. He stood at the edge of the
lamplight as if the lamplight were a border he was not certain he was allowed to cross, and
he was wet — not rain-wet, not river-wet, but marsh-wet, the kind of wet that comes from
lying in black water for a long time and then standing up. There was a wound at his throat,
old and poorly closed, and a second at his ribs that was still breathing.

"You're not from Quill's Hollow," she said — not a question.

"No," he said, and the word came out like something he had to carry.

She did not invite him in. She did not turn him away. She set the net down, opened the door
the rest of the way, and stepped back — and that, in the marsh, was as much an invitation
as anyone ever gave. He crossed the lamplight border like a man walking into deep water,
and the door closed behind him, and the reeds outside stayed perfectly, impossibly still.

She counted the wounds by lamplight while he sat without speaking. Eleven of them. Three
deep enough to worry about. The one at his throat had been made by something with a dull
edge, or with an edge that had been deliberately dulled — she could not tell which, and the
difference mattered. The one at his ribs was newer, and it was the one that was breathing.

"Hold still," she whispered. "This will sting."

He held still. It stung. He did not make a sound, and that was the second wrong thing —
wounded men who came through the marsh always made a sound when you cleaned a wound that
deep, unless they had practiced not to, and you did not practice not to unless you expected
to be wounded often, and you did not expect to be wounded often unless you were the kind of
person who was always going to be standing at the edge of someone's lamplight, wet and
bleeding, asking without asking to be let in.

Thunder rolled, far off, moving closer. The lamp guttered. The reeds outside were still
still.

She did not lead him toward the door. She led him away from it.
